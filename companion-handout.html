How to use this.

The digital workbook does the scenarios. This handout does the reflection — the part that needs your slowest, most honest writing. Three ways to use it:

1

Alongside the digital training.

Complete an entry on screen, then turn to the matching page here. The digital prompts are immediate; these are the ones to come back to in a quiet hour. Don't try to do all ten at once.

2

As a study guide for a small group.

Three to five colleagues, monthly. One entry per meeting. Everyone writes their answers privately first — fifteen minutes — then shares the one they're most willing to. The page keeps the conversation honest because nobody can wing it.

3

As a personal journal across the year.

Pick one page a month. Write the date in the corner. Come back next August and reread your old answers next to your new ones. That's the data on your own growth that no analytics tool can give you.

One note on whose voice is missing: these prompts often ask whose perspective the workbook didn't include — the parent, the student, the colleague, the building leader. That question is intentional. Fill it in honestly even when the honest answer is "I don't know yet, and that's the problem."

Discipline & behavior referrals

The 'disruptive' reader

01
From the entry Black students are referred for "subjective" infractions — disruption, defiance, disrespect — at 2-3x the rate of white peers for identical behaviors.
  • Whose voice was missing from this scenario?
  • Write about a real moment a colleague pushed you to discipline a student of color. What did you do? What do you wish you had done?
  • In the last month, how many of your referrals (or close calls) were students of color? List them by initial.
  • What is one structural change — not a personal intention — that would interrupt a referral pipeline in your building?
Classroom participation

Who gets called on

02
From the entry Teachers call on boys 80% more often than girls, and white students more than students of color — even when they believe they call on everyone equally.
  • If you tallied your call-ons for one period tomorrow, what pattern do you predict you'd find? Be specific by name.
  • When you skip a student because you "don't want to put them on the spot" — what are you actually protecting? Them, or you?
  • What kind of question do you usually ask your strongest students? Your quietest? Your most-likely-to-be-wrong students?
  • Pick one structural change you could try Monday (random caller, equity sticks, no-hands-up). What do you predict will be hard about it?
Staff lounge conversations

'That family, though'

03
From the entry Deficit framing of families of color — "they don't care," "they won't show up" — is the single strongest predictor of low teacher expectations.
  • Write down a sentence you've heard in your staff lounge about a family that, in retrospect, was deficit framing. Who said it? What did you do?
  • Why is interrupting a colleague harder than interrupting a student? What's actually at risk for you?
  • Draft a one-line interrupt you could realistically say next time. Make it short enough to remember on a tired Friday.
  • If a colleague used your interrupt against you ("oh, calling me racist now?"), what would you say next?
Parent-teacher conferences

Before Mr. Nguyen arrives

04
From the entry Asian American students are disproportionately described in trait-based language ("sweet," "quiet") rather than skill-based language — a pattern that mirrors how girls are described.
  • Pick a recent conference. Write down three things you said about the student. How many were trait language vs. skill language?
  • Whose conference notes are thin in your gradebook right now? What does that thinness tell you?
  • What assumptions do you make based on a parent's English fluency? Be honest. Name at least one.
  • Write a one-sentence opening line you could use to ask any parent — without performing — what they want you to know about their child.
Academic tracking & recommendations

The gifted referral form

05
From the entry Black students are 54% less likely than white students with the same test scores to be recommended for gifted programs by their teachers.
  • Look at your last list of academic recommendations — gifted, advanced math, summer enrichment, anything. Who's on it? Who isn't? Why?
  • Name a student you considered "smart but not gifted." Why? What would you have to see for that to change?
  • Whose family did you not contact about the opportunity? What story did you tell yourself about why?
  • If you had to add one student to a recommendation list right now, who would it be — and what's stopping you?
Special education referrals

'Something's off' with DeShawn

06
From the entry Black students are 40% more likely to be identified with emotional/behavioral disorders, and far less likely to be identified with specific learning disabilities, than white peers with similar profiles.
  • Think of a student you've referred — or considered referring — for SPED. Write about the moment you decided "something's off." What did you actually see?
  • What Tier 1 strategies had you tried, and for how long? Be honest about whether "I tried everything" was actually a few weeks.
  • How well do you know the student's home language and home life? List what you know. List what you assumed.
  • If the referral were declined, what would you do for this student instead? Write the actual plan.
Peer-to-peer conflicts

The playground scuffle

07
From the entry Black girls are viewed as less innocent and more adult-like than white girls of the same age, starting as early as age five — a pattern researchers call "adultification bias."
  • Recall a recent peer conflict in your class. Whose version did you believe first? Why?
  • When two students give competing accounts, which student's affect (calm, crying, angry, quiet) reads to you as "the truth-teller"? Why might that itself be biased?
  • Have you ever described a student of color, especially a Black girl, as "knows better" or "should know" — language you'd never use for a white peer her age? Write about it.
  • What's a script you could use to slow down the consequence-decision when a conflict has a racial layer?
Grading & feedback language

Reading the same essay twice

08
From the entry When the same student essay is presented with a white-sounding or Black-sounding name, teachers on average give the Black-named version a lower grade and more deficit-framed feedback.
  • Look at your last batch of graded student work. Re-read the comments you wrote on three students of color and three white students. What patterns do you see in your language?
  • When does your feedback get shorter? When does it get more critical? Whose work tends to fall in each category?
  • Could you grade your next set name-hidden? What would have to change about your process? What are you afraid you'd find?
  • Pick one feedback phrase you use a lot. Translate it from "deficit" to "strengths-and-next-steps." Practice writing the new version.
Quick bias check

First-word association

09
From the entry Over 85% of educators who take the Race IAT show some degree of implicit pro-white association — including the majority of educators of color.
  • When you imagined "the bright student" in your mind — before reading further — who came to mind? Race, gender, class. Be specific. No one is reading this but you.
  • Where did that image come from? Family? Schooling? Movies? Your own past students?
  • Have you taken a Project Implicit IAT? If yes, what did you score, and what did you do with the result? If no, what's stopping you?
  • What is one snap judgment you've made about a student in the last month that, on reflection, doesn't hold up?
Commitment & action

What are you taking with you?

10
From the entry Bias training without structural follow-through shows null effects at 90 days. Training plus one sustained structural change shows measurable shifts in referral and grading patterns.
  • What is the single most uncomfortable thing you wrote in this workbook? Write it again here, and circle the part you most want to forget.
  • Write three commitments. Specific. Behavioral. Time-bound. (Not "I'll be more aware." Yes "I will track call-ons in 3rd period for two weeks.")
  • Who is one colleague you will share these commitments with, by name? When will you tell them?
  • Pick a check-in date 30 days from now. Write it down. What will you ask yourself?
  • If nothing changes — if you do this workbook and your classroom looks the same in three months — what will that have meant?

Appendix A · Research

Every research clip from the workbook, with full source — for facilitators, skeptics, and curious educators.
Entry 01 · Discipline
"Black children represent 18% of preschool enrollment but 48% of preschool children receiving more than one out-of-school suspension."
U.S. Department of Education, Office for Civil Rights · Civil Rights Data Collection (CRDC), 2014 Snapshot. Available at ocrdata.ed.gov.
Entry 02 · Participation
"Teachers call on boys in class 80% more often than girls, and white students more often than students of color — even when they believe they call on everyone equally."
Sadker, D. & Zittleman, K. Still Failing at Fairness: How Gender Bias Cheats Girls and Boys in School and What We Can Do About It (Scribner, 2009).
Entry 03 · Staff lounge
"Deficit framing of families of color — 'they don't care,' 'they won't show up' — is the single strongest predictor of low teacher expectations."
Hammond, Z. Culturally Responsive Teaching and the Brain: Promoting Authentic Engagement and Rigor Among Culturally and Linguistically Diverse Students (Corwin, 2014).
Entry 04 · Conferences
"Asian American students are disproportionately described by teachers in trait-based language ('sweet,' 'quiet') rather than skill-based language."
Lee, S. J. Unraveling the "Model Minority" Stereotype: Listening to Asian American Youth, 2nd ed. (Teachers College Press, 2009).
Entry 05 · Tracking
"Black students are 54% less likely than white students with the same test scores to be recommended for gifted programs by their teachers."
Grissom, J. A. & Redding, C. (2016). "Discretion and Disproportionality: Explaining the Underrepresentation of High-Achieving Students of Color in Gifted Programs." AERA Open, 2(1).
Entry 06 · SPED
"Black students are 40% more likely to be identified with emotional/behavioral disorders, and far less likely with specific learning disabilities, than white peers with similar profiles."
National Center for Learning Disabilities. Significant Disproportionality in Special Education: Trends Among Black Students (2020). Available at ncld.org.
Entry 07 · Conflicts
"Black girls are viewed as less innocent and more adult-like than white girls of the same age, starting as early as age five — a pattern researchers call 'adultification bias.'"
Epstein, R., Blake, J., & González, T. (2017). Girlhood Interrupted: The Erasure of Black Girls' Childhood. Georgetown Law Center on Poverty and Inequality.
Entry 08 · Grading
"When the same student essay is presented with a white-sounding or Black-sounding name, teachers on average give the Black-named version a lower grade and more deficit-framed feedback."
Quinn, D. M. (2020). "Experimental Evidence on Teachers' Racial Bias in Student Evaluation." Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, 42(3).
Entry 09 · IAT
"Over 85% of educators who take the Race IAT show some degree of implicit pro-white association — including the majority of educators of color."
Project Implicit, Harvard University. Aggregate data from the Race Implicit Association Test, available at implicit.harvard.edu. See also Banaji, M. & Greenwald, A. Blindspot: Hidden Biases of Good People (Bantam, 2013).
Entry 10 · Action
"Bias training without structural follow-through shows null effects at 90 days. Training plus one sustained structural change shows measurable shifts in referral and grading patterns."
Forscher, P. S., Lai, C. K., Axt, J. R., et al. (2019). "A meta-analysis of procedures to change implicit measures." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 117(3).

Appendix B · Where to read next

If you read fifteen pages a week, you'll finish any one of these in a month. Pick the one whose cover speaks to you.

Start here — accessible, classroom-grounded

  • Zaretta Hammond. Culturally Responsive Teaching and the Brain. The book most cited by classroom teachers who actually changed their practice. Pairs neuroscience with concrete moves. Best for: anyone who wants the "what do I do Monday" version.
  • Bettina L. Love. We Want to Do More Than Survive. A polemic and a love letter to abolitionist teaching. Will reframe what you thought "good teaching" meant. Best for: educators ready to interrogate their assumptions about what schools are for.
  • Christopher Emdin. For White Folks Who Teach in the Hood... and the Rest of Y'all Too. Direct, funny, and full of practical reality-checks. Best for: anyone whose classroom does not match their own background.

Go deeper — the cognitive science of bias

  • Mahzarin Banaji & Anthony Greenwald. Blindspot: Hidden Biases of Good People. The book by the architects of the IAT. Disarmingly readable. Best for: skeptics who want to understand the underlying science.
  • Jennifer Eberhardt. Biased: Uncovering the Hidden Prejudice That Shapes What We See, Think, and Do. A Stanford psychologist's tour through the data, including a chapter on schools. Best for: anyone who likes their argument with research receipts.
  • Beverly Daniel Tatum. "Why Are All the Black Kids Sitting Together in the Cafeteria?" Updated edition. The foundational text on racial identity development in schools. Best for: educators in racially diverse buildings.

For voices the workbook didn't make room for

  • Gholdy Muhammad. Cultivating Genius: An Equity Framework for Culturally and Historically Responsive Literacy. A literacy framework rooted in the Black literary societies of the 1800s. Best for: K-5 ELA teachers.
  • Monique W. Morris. Pushout: The Criminalization of Black Girls in Schools. The book that gave many educators the language for what they'd been seeing happen to Black girls. Best for: anyone in a building with disproportionate referrals of girls of color.
  • Carla Shalaby. Troublemakers: Lessons in Freedom from Young Children at School. Four ethnographies of "difficult" first-graders. Will change how you see classroom canaries. Best for: K-2 teachers and anyone who has ever called a six-year-old a problem.

Online — free, no commitment

  • Project Implicit. Implicit Association Tests. implicit.harvard.edu. Take the Race IAT in fifteen minutes. Don't share your score; do sit with it. Free.
  • Learning for Justice (formerly Teaching Tolerance). learningforjustice.org. Lesson plans, framework articles, and the Social Justice Standards. Free, K-12.
  • EdWeek. "Where Implicit Bias Lives in Schools" series. A useful set of short, recent articles to keep on hand for staff conversations. Some paywall, much free.

A last note — These are starting points, not a syllabus. Your students, your colleagues, and your own attention will tell you what to read next better than any list can.
